ChartGPT Research Paper Summary

A summary of "ChartGPT: Leveraging LLMs to Generate Charts from Abstract Natural Language" by Yuan Tian et al., IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics. Read the paper.

Overview

Currently natural language interfaces are gaining popularity quickly and professionals are investigating the use of these natural language interfaces for the design of data visualizations. However, the challenge comes from accurately translating vague user requests into chart specifications and producing that chart accurately. Most often users do not specify everything in the way that an experienced data professional might. An experienced professional might, in their original prompt, ask the large language model to use a specific measure, chart, or aggregation level. Non-experts rarely specify these. This limits the general use of natural language interfaces to create data visualizations.

Furthermore, large language models suffer from two issues that make their use for data visualization creation from users’ requests more difficult: First, controlling chart parameters with a large language model is especially difficult because of the problem of hallucinations. While large language models are great at generating text, that text may not always be accurate. Secondly, large language models lack a straightforward way to inject data visualization knowledge into the model. These models are trained to generate text, not data visualizations. Professionals typically use prompting and fine-tuning to improve language models at visualization creation. Prompting is impractical, and fine-tuning is challenging because few suitable datasets exist that can be used to fine tune existing text-based generation models for data visualization generation.

Previous Research/ Methods

Existing research and methods fall into two categories: rule-based approaches and machine learning-based approaches. In rule-based approaches, they attempt to map data visualization knowledge to user requests. This is difficult because rules are inflexible and require substantial work to maintain them. The machine learning based approaches are more flexible, but they primarily focus on generating charts from data tables while this paper focuses on generating visualizations from user requests.

ChartGPT

The authors constructed their ChartGPT model by fine-tuning an existing model. To do this, they had to construct their own dataset which mapped “user utterances” to data visualizations. None of the existing datasets did this so the authors were forced to create their own. NvBench, a dataset of 25,000+ (data, request, chart) triplets, was close but not exactly what the authors wanted. NvBench's requests were written by data professionals and explicitly named chart types, columns, and aggregations, so the authors had an LLM rewrite them into ambiguous, human-like utterances. Fourteen human raters could not reliably tell the rewritten requests from human ones (56% error rate).

Once the dataset of triplets was constructed, the authors then fine-tuned the open-source FLAN-T5-XL model on the training set containing more than 1,200 triplets.

Interface

ChartGPT generates charts through a sequential pipeline based on the grammar of graphics, which splits the task into two stages of three subtasks each. The data transformation stage selects columns, filters the data, and applies any necessary aggregation. The visualization transformation stage chooses the chart type, encodes the data with marks and channels, and applies any necessary sorting. Each subtask passes its output to the next to strengthen the model's reasoning, and the answers are then combined into the final chart.

Figure 2 from Tian et al.: an overview of ChartGPT’s step-by-step pipeline, from the input table and request to the final chart.

Users upload their data and a simple request and receive three candidate charts. They can refine a chart in the configuration panel without rewriting the request.

Evaluation

The authors used their test set of approximately 300 triplets to test their ChartGPT software alongside NL4DV and ncNet (other natural language to visualization interfaces). The authors used two metrics to evaluate the effectiveness of their model since ambiguous user requests can have more than one correct answer. They used consistency and similarity scores. Consistency was achieved when charts generated by the various approaches were identical to their ground truth answers. Similarity was measured by converting each of the generated charts to a text description of the visualizations and then using the ROUGE-L and BLEU algorithms to measure similarity.

Figure 8a from Tian et al.: average user scores for charts from ChartGPT, ncNet, and NL4DV in the comparative study.

The results of the evaluation suggest that ChartGPT outperforms existing natural language to visualization models on both the consistency and similarity metrics. When the authors investigated the reasons for ChartGPT’s performance over the other two approaches they found that two things were responsible for the improved performance. ChartGPT was better at understanding ambiguous user requests and filling in omitted details from the request using chain-of-thought reasoning.

User Study

The authors conducted a comparative study to understand users’ preferences of the charts generated by ChartGPT against ncNet and NL4DV. Users scored the charts generated by the three different approaches from 0-3. The results showed that users preferred the charts generated by ChartGPT. ChartGPT averaged 2.25, while ncNet and NL4DV averaged 0.817 and 0.908.

The authors also conducted a usability study in which users selected a dataset and attempted to use ChartGPT to generate visualizations. The results showed that the software was very effective at aiding users in generating their preferred data visualizations. In 49 of the 53 attempts, users were able to successfully generate their preferred chart. Of those 49 successful attempts, 23 were generated on the first attempt. The qualitative results of the usability study also indicated that the model was effective. Many users submitted vague requests and were able to generate their preferred visualization. The model’s enhanced understanding allowed the users to express themselves more naturally. They were able to use natural language and still have the model select the correct specifications. Furthermore, the configuration interface allowed users to dial in the generated visualization to better match their expectations.

Limitations

The study has a few limitations worth noting. The training requests were rewritten by a language model, not written by real users, so they may not capture how people actually phrase questions. The test set was small, around 300 examples, which makes it hard to know how well the results generalize. The consistency score only counts exact matches, but a vague request can have more than one good answer, so it may undercount charts that are actually reasonable. And the user studies were fairly small, so the strong preference for ChartGPT should be confirmed with larger and more varied groups of users.

Conclusion

The authors of this paper showed strong evidence that natural language interfaces can be made to be more effective at generating data visualizations. They also created a dataset that can be used to benchmark future research. Lastly, they developed a framework of decomposing a large problem into smaller ones that can be extended to other domains beyond data visualization generation.

My Takeaways

What stood out to me most was the step-by-step pipeline. It follows the same order I use when I build a dashboard by hand: pick the fields, filter, aggregate, choose a chart, and sort. Breaking the problem into small steps seems like a practical way to cut down on hallucinations. I'm less convinced that training on machine-written requests fully reflects real users, and I'd like to see it tested on messy, real business questions. Still, being able to adjust one step in the configuration panel instead of rewriting the whole request feels like a design that would actually help analysts and non-experts in their day-to-day work.

Discussion Questions

  1. Should a chart tool ask a clarifying question when a request is vague, or should it just make its best guess?
  2. The training requests were written by GPT-3. What risks come from training a model on machine-written data?
  3. Is exact match a fair way to grade answers to vague questions? How would you grade them instead?
  4. In your own dashboard work, when would you value inspiration over accuracy?
